## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard takes a piece of writing and tells the reader whether it was
probably written by a person, probably generated by AI, or too close to call.
It never sees who wrote it. All it has are two measurements of the text, and
the writer can always appeal.

### The path a submission takes

1. **The request arrives at `POST /submit`** (`app.py`). It brings `text` and
   `creator_id` as JSON. Nothing has been judged yet.
2. **The route checks the input.** Missing, empty, too-short or oversized text
   is refused with a 400 and an error code, and the refusal goes in the audit
   log. This step adds a guarantee that everything downstream gets text it can
   actually score.
3. **Signal one, `detector.model_signal(text)`, scores predictability.** GPT-2
   measures how predictable the text is (perplexity), squashed to 0–1, where
   higher means more AI-like. This step adds a reading of the *words*.
4. **Signal two, `stylometry.style_signal(text)`, scores shape.** It measures
   the text's shape: how much sentence length varies, how much vocabulary
   repeats, and how much and what kind of punctuation it uses. That gives a
   second 0–1 score, also higher = more AI-like. This step adds a reading of
   the *form*, independent of meaning.
5. **`scoring.combine_signals` combines the two scores into one.** This is
   where my rule for disagreement between the signals lives, and where I
   decide which way I'd rather be wrong.
6. **`scoring.score_to_label` turns the number into words.** The combined score
   becomes a guess (`human` / `unsure` / `ai`) and a sentence written for
   someone who has never heard of a threshold.
7. **The decision is logged and returned.** `audit.log_decision` writes all
   nine fields to `logs/audit.jsonl`, both signal scores included. The
   response hands the writer a `content_id`, which is the only thing they need
   to appeal through `POST /appeal`. An appeal moves the item to
   `under_review` without overwriting the original decision.

### Following a false positive through it

A real writer submits work they wrote themselves, and the service calls it AI.

**Who they are, most likely:** someone writing in a second language, or anyone
who writes plainly on purpose. They write short, ordinary sentences in common
words they're sure of.

**What score they get:**
- **Signal one:** GPT-2 finds common words in ordinary sentences easy to
  predict, so the model score comes out high, around 0.7–0.8. That's the same
  range as actual machine text.
- **Signal two:** sentences of similar length with a modest vocabulary and
  little punctuation beyond full stops. That's exactly the "even, narrow"
  shape it reads as AI, so the style score is likely 0.6 or above.
- **Combined:** both signals make the same mistake for the same reason, so
  combining them doesn't cancel it out. They agree with each other, and
  they're both wrong. With the starter's 0.5/0.5 weights and 0.65 AI
  threshold, that writer lands at about 0.7 and gets the high-confidence AI
  label.

**What the label says to them:** whatever I write for the high-confidence AI
label. If it reads like a verdict ("This was generated by AI"), it's an
accusation against someone who has the least room to argue back.

**What they can do about it:** `POST /appeal` with their `content_id` and a
reason. The item moves to `under_review`, the appeal sits in the log beside
the original decision, and nothing is re-scored automatically. A person has to
look. That only works if the response gave them the `content_id` and the label
told them an appeal exists.

**What this decides:** the AI threshold has to sit above where plain human
writing lands, even at the cost of letting some AI text through as `unsure`.
The AI label should describe what the measurement found, not what the writer
did, and it should point to the appeal. I'll check where a plainly written
human paragraph actually scores when I run the calibration inputs in
Milestone 4, and set thresholds from that, not from the defaults.

### The contract

**`POST /submit`**

Request (JSON):

| Field | Type | Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `text` | string | yes | The writing to check. At least a couple of sentences; at most 20,000 characters. GPT-2 only reads the first ~300 words (400 tokens) anyway. |
| `creator_id` | string | yes | Whoever is submitting. Not used in scoring; used for the log, appeals, and unit 8's rate limit. |

Response `200`:

| Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `content_id` | string (UUID) | This submission's id. The only thing you need to appeal. |
| `guess` | `"human"` \| `"unsure"` \| `"ai"` | Machine-readable verdict. |
| `confidence` | float 0–1 | How sure the service is of `guess`: the combined score for `ai`, 1 − combined score for `human`, and for `unsure`, how close the combined score is to the middle (1 − 2·\|score − 0.5\|). |
| `label` | string | The full sentence a reader sees. |
| `model_score` | float 0–1 | Signal one. Higher = more AI-like. |
| `style_score` | float 0–1 | Signal two. Higher = more AI-like. |
| `combined_score` | float 0–1 | Output of `combine_signals`. Higher = more AI-like. |
| `status` | `"decided"` | Becomes `under_review` after an appeal. |

Errors, always `{"error": "<code>", "message": "<sentence a person can act on>"}`:

| Status | `error` | When |
|---|---|---|
| 400 | `invalid_json` | Body isn't a JSON object. |
| 400 | `missing_text` / `missing_creator_id` | Field absent or not a string. |
| 400 | `empty_text` | Text is only whitespace. |
| 400 | `text_too_short` | Too few tokens for the model to score (`detector.perplexity` raises). |
| 413 | `text_too_long` | Over 20,000 characters. |
| 429 | `rate_limited` | Unit 8. |
| 503 | `detector_unavailable` | The model couldn't load. |

Every refusal is also written to the audit log with `audit.log_rejection`.

**`POST /appeal`**

Request (JSON):

| Field | Type | Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `content_id` | string | yes | From the `/submit` response. |
| `reasoning` | string | yes | The writer's explanation, non-empty. |

The `creator_id` for the log entry is taken from the original decision, not
from the request, so nobody can file an appeal under someone else's name.

Response `200`:

| Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `content_id` | string | Echoed back. |
| `status` | `"under_review"` | The new status. |
| `message` | string | Confirmation for the writer that a person will look at it. |

Errors: `400 missing_content_id` / `missing_reasoning`, `404 unknown_content_id`
(no decision with that id in the log), and `409 already_under_review` (a
second appeal on the same item).

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2, running locally.
`detector.py` computes perplexity, which is the model's average surprise per
token over the first 400 tokens, then maps it onto 0–1 so that low perplexity
(predictable) scores high (AI-like). A perplexity of 45 scores 0.5.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** a language model
writes by repeatedly choosing a likely next word, so its output is text a
model finds easy to predict. People make odder choices: a specific detail
instead of the typical one, an abrupt turn, a word nobody expected.

**What it can't see:** who wrote the text. Predictability isn't authorship.
What this signal really rewards is common words in short, ordinary sentences,
which is also how second-language writers, younger writers and anyone writing
plainly on purpose write. It scores them as machine-like. In the other
direction, ornate prose full of rare words reads as human, so AI text prompted
to "use unusual vocabulary" can slip past. It also only reads the first ~300
words.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text, without reading a word of it. It
combines three measures: how much sentence length varies (standard deviation
over mean), how much of the vocabulary repeats (type-token ratio), and how much
punctuation there is and what kind (commas, semicolons, dashes). These go onto
a 0–1 score, with higher = more AI-like.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** "most likely next
word", averaged over a whole passage, produces even, mid-length sentences, a
narrow band of vocabulary and tidy punctuation. Human writing is lumpier: a
three-word sentence next to a forty-word one, a stray dash, a repeated pet
word.

**What it can't see:** meaning. Anything that changes the shape without
changing the substance moves it. Typos raise vocabulary variety, splitting or
merging sentences raises length spread, and pasting in a quotation adds
punctuation. Each of those is a cheap, deliberate edit that makes AI text look
human. It's also unreliable on short text: one sentence has no spread at all,
and type-token ratio falls as text gets longer, so length alone can look like
a signal.

**Where the two blind spots overlap:** plain, even, simply worded human
writing looks AI-like to *both* signals. A second signal doesn't protect that
writer, because the two signals make the same mistake on the same text.
